What is drip irrigation?

Updated 16 Aug 2026 · 8 min read
Short answer

Drip irrigation is a system that delivers water slowly and directly to the root zone through emitters spaced along a tube, rather than spraying it across the surface. Because almost nothing is lost to evaporation or drift, over 90% of applied water reaches the plant against 50–70% for overhead sprinklers.

Application efficiency is the share of applied water that reaches the root zone rather than evaporating or drifting away.

Drip irrigation is a way of watering that puts water directly into the root zone, slowly, through emitters spaced along a tube — instead of throwing it across the field and hoping enough lands where the roots are.

That single difference is why it is the most water-efficient method in common use. Over 90% of applied water reaches the plant, against 50–70% for overhead sprinklers, and Penn State's typical figures put trickle at 95% against sprinklers at 70%.

Drip irrigation, defined

The short definition: drip irrigation applies water slowly and directly to the root zone through emitters, at low pressure, so that almost none of it is lost to the air or to ground the crop is not using. Everything else — tape or line, surface or buried, timed or manual — is a variation inside that sentence.

How drip irrigation works

Water enters the system at the source — a tap, a well, a pump or a tank — and passes through a filter, then a pressure regulator, then along a mainline to the rows. Along each row runs drip tape or tubing with emitters built in at fixed spacing.

Each emitter releases only a few liters an hour. At that rate water does not pool or run off; it soaks down and outward, forming a wetted zone around the roots. The soil does the distributing, which is why soil texture matters so much: sand spreads the water narrowly and deep, clay spreads it wide and shallow.

The whole system runs at low pressure, typically 8–12 psi. Household and pump pressure is several times that, which is the single most important thing to understand before buying anything.

What a drip irrigation system is made of

Five components, and the order is not negotiable.

  1. Water source. Tap, well, pond or tank. Its cleanliness decides your filtration.
  2. Filter. Emitter openings are measured in thousandths of an inch, and anything the water carries ends up there. This goes first.
  3. Pressure regulator. Brings supply pressure down to what the tape is rated for. Grit destroys regulator seats, which is why the filter comes before it and not after.
  4. Mainline. Solid tubing carrying water to the head of each row.
  5. Drip tape or tubing. The line with emitters that runs along the plants.

Tape and tubing are not the same thing. Drip tape is thin-walled, cheap per foot, and designed as a consumable — one to five seasons depending on wall thickness. Drip tubing is thicker, costs more, and lasts years; it suits permanent plantings such as orchards where you are not lifting the line every season.

What drip irrigation costs

Hardware for a garden-sized block starts in the low hundreds, and tape itself is cents per foot. The number that surprises people is not the first purchase but the replacement cycle.

Filters, regulators, mainline and fittings are bought once and last years. Tape is consumed. Budget it annually or every few years rather than treating the first order as the cost of the system — the full breakdown with current prices is in what irrigation actually costs.

Per-acre figures are misleading here, because cost scales with row spacing rather than area. An acre of orchard rows twelve feet apart needs roughly 3,600 feet of line; an acre of beds at three feet needs four times that.

Where drip is the wrong choice

It is not the answer to everything, and the honest cases against it are specific.

  • Dirty water. Pond water or iron-bearing wells clog emitters. Filtration stops being a $40 accessory and becomes the design constraint.
  • Broadcast plantings and pasture. Drip needs a line to follow. Grass and cover crops have no rows.
  • Mechanical cultivation. A hoe goes through tape without the operator noticing.
  • Frost protection. Sprinklers can carry a crop through a frost event by releasing latent heat as water freezes. Drip cannot do this at all.

Sizing a system for your own ground

The part most guides skip is that watering interval is a soil question before it is a crop question. Sand holds roughly 0.05 inches of plant-available water per inch of depth; silt loam holds about 0.18 — more than three times as much. The same crop on sand may need water daily and on loam every three or four days.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

What is a drip irrigation system made of?

Five parts in a fixed order: a water source, a filter, a pressure regulator, a mainline, and drip tape or tubing with emitters. The order matters more than the brand — the filter goes ahead of the regulator, and the regulator ahead of any tape. Reverse the first two and grit reaches the regulator seat; skip the regulator and the tape splits at the seam.

What is a drip irrigation system, in one sentence?

A drip irrigation system is a filter, a pressure regulator and a network of tubing with emitters that delivers water at low pressure straight to the root zone — the whole assembly, as opposed to the tape alone.

How does drip irrigation work?

Water is delivered under low pressure — typically 8–12 psi — through tubing laid along the row. Emitters spaced at intervals meter it out a few liters per hour each, so it soaks into the root zone instead of running off or evaporating. The soil, not the pipe, does the distributing.

Is drip irrigation better than sprinklers?

On water use, decisively: over 90% application efficiency against 50–70% overhead, and Penn State puts the typical comparison at 95% against 70%. On everything else it depends — drip needs clean water, suits fixed plant spacing, and is destroyed by a hoe. Sprinklers handle dirty water, irregular plantings and frost protection.

How much does drip irrigation cost?

Hardware for a garden block starts in the low hundreds, and tape itself costs cents per foot. The cost that catches people out is not the first purchase but the replacement cycle: tape lasts one to five seasons depending on wall thickness, while filters, regulators and fittings last years.

What is drip tape?

Thin-walled tubing with emitters built in at set spacing, sold in rolls and laid along the row. It is the consumable part of a drip system — designed to be replaced — as opposed to drip tubing, which is thicker, more expensive and lasts far longer.

Can drip irrigation be used for a lawn?

Poorly. A lawn needs the whole surface wetted evenly, which is exactly what drip does not do — it wets a line of root zones. Subsurface drip lines exist for turf, but for most people a sprinkler is the right tool for grass and drip is the right tool for rows and beds.
