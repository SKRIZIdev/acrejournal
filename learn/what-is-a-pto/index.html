What is a PTO on a tractor?

Updated 16 Aug 2026 · 7 min read ·Checked against manufacturer specifications and extension safety material
Short answer

The power take-off is a splined shaft, usually at the rear of the tractor, that sends engine power to an implement instead of to the wheels. Two standard speeds exist — 540 and 1000 rpm — and the implement must match. The horsepower that reaches the shaft is roughly 15% below the engine figure on the brochure.

A tractor is not simply a vehicle that pulls things. What makes it a tractor is that it can send engine power somewhere other than the wheels — to a shaft that drives a mower, a tiller, a baler, a post-hole auger. That shaft is the power take-off.

What does PTO mean on a tractor?

PTO stands for power take-off. On a tractor it means the splined shaft — and the gearing behind it — that takes power off the engine and sends it to an implement instead of to the wheels. When someone says a tractor "has 19.8 PTO horsepower", they mean that is what arrives at that shaft, not what the engine produces.

That is the whole distinction that makes a tractor a tractor rather than a vehicle that tows things.

What the shaft is

A splined stub, usually at the rear, sometimes also mid-mounted for a belly mower. A driveline connects it to the implement's gearbox. When the PTO is engaged, the shaft turns whenever the engine does — which is the whole point and also the danger, and why every guard and shield on that driveline exists.

Live or independent PTO means the shaft keeps turning when you press the clutch to change gear. Almost everything modern in the compact class is independent; on older machines, stopping the tractor stopped the implement, which mattered when the implement was a baler part-way through a bale.

Why two speeds exist

540 and 1000 rpm are the two standards, and they are not interchangeable.

At full recommended speed a 540 shaft turns about nine times a second, and a 1000 rpm shaft about 16.6 times a second. Implements are engineered around one of those inputs: gear ratios, blade tip speed, pump displacement all assume a specific shaft speed.

The industry stops you mixing them up mechanically — the 1000 rpm shaft carries more splines, so a 540 implement's driveline will not slide onto it. That is a deliberate safety design rather than an inconvenience.

For compact tractors, 540 is what you will use. 1000 rpm shows up on larger machinery and on implements with high continuous draw.

Some tractors also offer economy modes, marked 540E and 1000E. These deliver the standard shaft speed at lower engine revs, saving fuel and noise on light work. The implement still sees a standard speed; only the engine works less hard.

The horsepower that actually arrives

This is the part that costs people money.

The horsepower quoted on a brochure is gross engine horsepower, measured at the flywheel. The implement never sees that. Between the engine and the shaft sit the transmission and the drivetrain, and each takes its share.

Kubota's L2502 makes the loss concrete. Gross engine output is 23.3 hp. At the PTO it delivers 19.8 hp with the 8F/8R gear transmission — a loss of about 15% — and 18.4 hp with the hydrostatic, about 21%.

Two consequences follow.

Size implements against the PTO figure. A tool that needs 20 PTO horsepower is marginal on this tractor and impossible on its hydrostatic version, even though the engine number looks comfortable.

The transmission choice costs power. Hydrostatic is easier to operate and gives up 1.4 hp at the shaft on this machine. For loader work that trade is usually worth it; for running a tiller at the edge of its requirement, it is not.

Matching an implement, in order

  1. Hitch category. Category I pins are 7/8 inch, Category II are 1 1/8 inch. Get this wrong and the tool does not mount.
  2. PTO speed. 540 or 1000. The splines will tell you, but check before the delivery truck arrives.
  3. PTO horsepower available, not engine horsepower.
  4. Lift capacity, if the implement hangs on the three-point hitch — and remember the rating is quoted at a set distance behind the hitch, with capacity falling as the load moves back.
  5. Type of load. A rotary cutter works in bursts and can sit near your power limit. A tiller or a subsoiler pulls continuously and should sit well under it.

One safety note that is not optional

An unguarded rotating PTO shaft is the most dangerous thing on a small farm, and the injuries it causes are not near-misses. Extension services publish this material for a reason. Do not run a driveline with a missing shield, do not step over a turning shaft, and disengage the PTO before leaving the seat — every time, including the time it is quicker not to.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

What does PTO stand for on a tractor?

Power take-off. It is the shaft that takes power off the engine and sends it to an implement rather than to the wheels — which is what separates a tractor from a vehicle that merely tows things.

What is PTO on a tractor used for?

Driving anything that needs rotating power rather than pulling: a rotary cutter, a tiller, a post-hole auger, a baler, a snow blower. If an implement has moving parts of its own, it runs off the PTO; if it only needs to be dragged or lifted, it does not.

540 or 1000 rpm — which do I need?

Whatever the implement is built for. 540 rpm is the standard for implements sized to compact and utility tractors; 1000 rpm belongs to larger machinery with higher power draw. At full recommended speed a 540 shaft turns nine times a second and a 1000 shaft about 16.6 times a second.

Can I run a 1000 rpm implement at 540?

No, and the design prevents you from trying by accident: the spline profiles differ, with more splines on the 1000 rpm shaft. An implement engineered for one input speed will not perform at the other, and driving a 540 implement at 1000 rpm is a way to destroy its gearbox quickly.

What are 540E and 1000E?

Economy modes. They deliver the same shaft speed at lower engine rpm, which saves fuel and noise on light work. They are not a separate standard — the implement still sees 540 or 1000 at the shaft.

Why is PTO horsepower lower than engine horsepower?

Because the drivetrain takes its share on the way. A Kubota L2502 with a 23.3 hp engine delivers 19.8 hp at the PTO with the gear transmission and 18.4 with hydrostatic — a loss of roughly 15% and 21% respectively. Implements are sized against the PTO figure, not the engine one.
