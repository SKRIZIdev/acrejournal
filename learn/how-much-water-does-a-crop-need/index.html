How much water does a crop actually need?

Updated 16 Aug 2026 · 6 min read
Short answer

Start with what your soil holds. Sand stores roughly 0.05 inches of plant-available water per inch of depth, silt loam about 0.18 — more than three times as much. Multiply by rooting depth to get the reservoir, then divide by daily use to get the interval. The crop matters less than most people expect; the soil matters more.

Ask how often to water and you will get an answer in days. It is the wrong unit, because the same crop on two soils a mile apart needs two different schedules.

The right way round is to work out how much water your ground can hold, how deep the roots go to get it, and how fast the crop spends it. Everything else — drip or sprinkler, timer settings, zone layout — follows from that.

The reservoir

Soil holds water in the pore spaces between particles, and the size of those spaces depends on texture. Sand has large pores that drain freely; clay and silt hold water tightly. What matters for irrigation is the fraction that plants can actually extract, called plant-available water.

The spread across common soils is large:

Soil texture Plant-available water
Sand about 0.05 in per inch of depth
Sandy loam around 0.11 in per inch
Silt loam about 0.18 in per inch

A silt loam holds more than three times what sand does. That single fact drives everything downstream: the same crop, in the same weather, needs water three times more often on the sand.

Rooting depth turns capacity into a reservoir

Capacity is per inch. What you actually have is capacity multiplied by the depth roots occupy.

A shallow-rooted salad crop working the top eight inches of a silt loam has roughly 1.4 inches of water available to it. A deep-rooted crop reaching two feet on the same ground has closer to 4.3 inches. Same soil, three times the buffer.

This is why leafy crops seem thirsty and established perennials seem tough. It is not mostly about the plant's water use — it is about how much of the soil's storage each one can reach.

What the crop spends

Daily use varies with the weather far more than with the species: hot, dry, windy days move water out of leaves and soil quickly, and cool cloudy ones barely at all. Crop stage matters too — a canopy at full cover uses much more than seedlings on bare ground.

For planning purposes, work with a peak-season daily figure rather than an average, since the schedule has to survive the worst week, not the typical one.

Putting it together

The arithmetic is simple once the three numbers exist:

Reservoir = water holding capacity × rooting depth Interval = usable share of that reservoir ÷ daily use

The usable share is not the whole reservoir. Plants begin to suffer well before the soil is empty, so irrigation scheduling normally refills after some fraction is spent — often around half — rather than letting the profile run dry.

Our drip calculator does this with your actual soil rather than a textbook average: it takes a ZIP code, finds the mapped soil series from the USDA survey, pulls its water holding capacity and returns the interval for your rooting depth. Checked against four states, it produces what you would expect — daily watering on Florida sand, every fourth day on Iowa loam.

What no calculator can tell you

It does not know your weather this week, your wind exposure, your water quality, or that half the block is in the shade of a treeline until noon.

Use the interval as a starting point, then adjust from what the crop and the soil tell you. Dig down and feel the soil at rooting depth before changing the schedule — it is a more reliable instrument than anything on the surface.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

Why does soil type matter more than the crop?

Because soil sets the size of the reservoir. A crop on sand and the same crop on silt loam use water at a similar rate, but the sand holds around a third as much of it — so the sand plot runs dry three times sooner. Changing crop changes the demand a little; changing soil changes the storage a lot.

How do I find out what my soil is?

The USDA soil survey has mapped essentially the whole country and the data is public. Our drip calculator queries it directly: give it a ZIP code and it returns the mapped soil series and its water holding capacity, then converts that into an interval.

Does rooting depth change the answer?

Substantially. The reservoir is water holding capacity multiplied by the depth roots actually occupy. Shallow-rooted salad crops draw on two or three times less stored water than a deep-rooted crop on the same ground, which is why they need watering more often, not more heavily.

Should I water little and often, or deeply and rarely?

Deep and less often, within what the soil can hold. Light frequent watering wets only the surface and encourages roots to stay there, which makes the crop more vulnerable the first time you miss a day. The exception is genuinely sandy ground, where the reservoir is too small to do anything else.

What does the calculator not know?

Your weather, your wind, your water salinity and what your crop did last week. It models the soil side accurately and the rest generically. Treat it as the starting interval and adjust from what you see in the field.
