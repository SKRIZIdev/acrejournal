Drip or sprinkler for a small holding

Updated 16 Aug 2026 · min read
At a glance

The efficiency numbers

Application efficiency is the share of applied water that reaches the root zone rather than evaporating or drifting.

The efficiency argument is settled and it is not the whole argument. Drip puts over 90% of applied water into the root zone; overhead sprinklers manage 50–70%, and Penn State's typical figures are 95% against 70%. If water is metered, scarce, or coming from a well with a slow recovery rate, that gap decides the question before anything else is considered.

Everywhere else, the decision turns on five things the efficiency number says nothing about.

1. Plant geometry

Drip works along a line. Rows, beds, orchard alleys, anything with fixed spacing — the tape runs beside the plants and every emitter has a job.

Broadcast plantings, pasture and cover crops have no line to follow. Running drip through them means either a grid of tape at absurd cost or accepting that most of the ground gets nothing. Sprinklers do not care about geometry; they wet a circle.

2. Water quality

This is the constraint people underestimate most.

Emitter openings are measured in thousandths of an inch. Everything the water carries — sand, silt, iron precipitate, algae — ends up there. On municipal water this is a $40 filter. On pond water or an iron-bearing well it is a filtration system that has to be maintained, and clogging is the failure that kills more drip installations than anything else.

Sprinklers pass debris that would block an emitter instantly. If your source is dirty and you are not prepared to filter it properly, overhead is the honest choice.

3. Cultivation

A hoe goes through drip tape without the operator feeling it. So does a cultivator, a rotary tiller, and a dog. The tear is often not obvious — you notice when the far end of the run stops delivering.

Where the ground gets worked mechanically during the season, either bury the line deep enough to be out of reach, or use overhead.

4. Frost protection

Sprinklers can carry a crop through a frost event: as water freezes on the plant it releases latent heat and holds the tissue near 32°F while the ice remains wet. It is a technique with real limits and it needs continuous application, but it works.

Drip cannot do this at all. If frost protection is part of why you are irrigating, the decision is made.

5. Labor and attention

Drip is quiet and invisible, which cuts both ways. It does not need supervision while it runs, but a blocked emitter or a split line is also invisible until the plants show it. Walking the rows to inspect emitters is a real recurring task.

Sprinklers announce their own faults — you can see a head that is not turning from across the field.

What most small holdings actually do

Both, on different ground, sharing a filter and a mainline.

Drip goes in the beds and permanent rows where spacing is fixed and water is expensive to waste. One or two sprinklers cover the irregular ground, the germination phase where you need to wet a whole surface rather than a line of root zones, and the frost nights.

That is not a compromise; it is matching the tool to the block. The drip calculator will size the drip half from your soil series, and what irrigation costs covers what each side of the system runs to.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

Which uses less water?

Drip, by a wide margin. Application efficiency is over 90% for drip against 50–70% for overhead sprinklers, and Penn State puts the typical comparison at 95% against 70%. On a metered supply or a limited well that difference can decide the whole question.

Which is cheaper to install?

For a small block, drip — tape costs cents per foot and a starter kit runs in the low hundreds. Over a larger irregular area sprinklers usually win, because drip needs a line along every row and sprinklers cover ground indiscriminately.

Can I run both?

Most small holdings end up doing exactly that. Drip in the beds and rows where the spacing is fixed, sprinklers for germination, for irregular ground and for frost protection. They share a filter and a mainline; only the zone hardware differs.

Which survives cultivation?

Sprinklers. Drip tape is destroyed by a hoe or a cultivator without the operator noticing, and a torn line loses pressure for everything downstream of it.

Which handles dirty water?

Sprinklers, easily. Drip emitters have openings measured in thousandths of an inch and clog on anything the water carries. With pond or iron-bearing well water, filtration stops being an accessory and becomes the design constraint.
