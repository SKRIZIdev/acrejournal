What is drip tape, and which wall thickness do you need?

Updated 17 Aug 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

Drip tape is thin-walled flexible tubing with emitters moulded inside at a fixed spacing. It lies flat when empty and rounds out under pressure. Thickness is quoted in mils — 6 mil is one or two seasons, 15 mil will run for several — and it works at 8–12 psi, which is why a pressure regulator is required rather than optional.

Drip tape is the cheapest way to put water exactly where a crop can reach it. It is thin polyethylene tubing, flat on the roll, with emitters moulded into the wall at a fixed spacing. Under pressure it rounds out and every emitter delivers roughly the same amount.

Everything about choosing it comes down to three numbers: wall thickness, emitter spacing, and the flow rate per hundred feet.

Tape or line

These get used interchangeably in shops and they are not the same product.

Drip tape is thin-walled, sold by the mil, and semi-disposable. It collapses flat when the water is off. It is designed for rows that get planted, harvested and cleared.

Drip line is thicker round tubing with emitters welded inside, stiff enough to hold its shape dry, and priced accordingly. It is designed to stay where you put it for years — orchards, hedges, permanent beds, landscaping.

If the ground gets tilled every year, buy tape. If it does not, line will outlive several rolls of tape and cost less over that period.

Wall thickness is a lifespan, not a quality

Tape is quoted in mils — thousandths of an inch. 6 mil is thin as a shopping bag; 15 mil feels like hose.

Thickness buys survival, not performance. A 6 mil tape and a 15 mil tape with the same emitter spacing water identically in their first season. The difference shows up when a stone presses against the wall, a rodent takes an interest, or ice forms inside it over winter.

The practical rule: pick the thinnest tape that survives your ground and your habits. One season of annual vegetables on clean soil, pulled up in autumn — 6 mil. Two or three seasons — 8 to 10 mil. Left in place, stony ground, or a rodent problem — 15 mil.

Emitter spacing follows the soil as much as the crop

Water leaving an emitter spreads as it sinks, and how far sideways it spreads depends on the soil. Sand holds almost nothing against gravity — the wetted zone is narrow, so emitters have to be close together or the plants between them get nothing. Loam and clay pull water sideways, so wider spacing still gives a continuous wetted band.

That is why the same crop takes different tape in Florida sand and Iowa loam. Our drip calculator reads the actual soil for a ZIP code from the USDA soil survey and works the interval out from its water-holding capacity, rather than assuming.

Flow rate is what sizes the system

Tape is rated in gallons per minute per hundred feet at its design pressure. That figure, multiplied by how many hundred feet of tape you intend to run at once, is your required supply. Get it wrong and the far end of the row runs at a lower pressure than the near end, which is exactly what a pressure-compensating tape or a shorter run length is there to fix.

Two things follow from this. Long rows need either compensating emitters or splitting into zones. And the filter is not optional: emitters are small enough that a grain of sand ends the life of one permanently, and there is no cleaning it out.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

What is drip tape?

Thin-walled polyethylene tubing with emitters built into the wall at a fixed spacing, usually 8, 12 or 16 inches. Water enters at low pressure and leaves through those emitters at a known rate per hundred feet. It is sold on rolls, laid down the crop row, and replaced when it fails rather than repaired.

What is the difference between drip tape and drip line?

Drip tape is thin-walled and flat when empty, sold by the mil, and treated as semi-disposable. Drip line is thicker-walled round tubing with emitters welded inside, stiffer, more expensive, and intended to stay in place for years. Row crops get tape; perennials, orchards and permanent beds get line.

What wall thickness of drip tape should I buy?

6 mil for a single season on annual vegetables where the tape will be pulled up at the end of the year. 8 to 10 mil where you want two or three seasons and the ground is reasonably clean. 15 mil where there are stones, insects or rodents, or where the tape is left in place over winter. Thicker tape does not water better — it just survives longer.

What emitter spacing do I need?

Match it to the crop spacing and the soil. Close spacing (8 inches) suits carrots, onions and anything sown in a continuous band, and sandy soil where water goes straight down. Wider spacing (12 to 16 inches) suits tomatoes, peppers and squash, and heavier soil where the wetted zone spreads sideways on its own.

How long does drip tape last?

One to five seasons, and the wall thickness plus how it is stored decides which. Sun on empty tape and freezing water inside it are what finish it. Tape drained and rolled up dry lasts materially longer than tape left in the field.

Do I need a pressure regulator for drip tape?

Yes. Tape runs at 8 to 12 psi and mains pressure is several times that — the first thing that happens without a regulator is that the tape splits at the seam. Filter and regulator are the two parts a drip system cannot be assembled without.
