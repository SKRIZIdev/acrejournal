Irrigation is the one system on a small holding where the cheap mistake and the expensive mistake look identical on the day you install them. Both versions water the field. The difference shows up in the second month, when half the emitters on one of them have stopped.

This section is built around that gap — not "drip is efficient", which everyone says, but the decisions that actually change the outcome: what your soil holds, what order the parts go in, and which components are consumables rather than fixtures.

Start with the soil, not the catalogue

The most common planning error is sizing a system around the crop and the acreage while treating soil as a detail. It is the other way round. Soil texture sets how much water the root zone can hold at all, and that number decides the watering interval before any equipment is chosen.

The spread is not subtle. A sandy soil holds roughly 0.05 inches of plant-available water per inch of depth; a silt loam holds around 0.18. Same crop, same weather, same emitter — and one field needs water daily while the other can wait four days. Size a timer for the wrong one and you either drown the crop or watch it wilt between cycles.

This does not have to be guesswork. The USDA soil survey has mapped essentially the whole country and the data is open. Our drip calculator takes a ZIP code, finds the mapped soil series underneath it, pulls the water holding capacity for that series and turns it into a watering interval for your crop's rooting depth.

Drip or sprinkler

Efficiency is the argument everyone leads with, and it is real: over 90% of water applied through drip reaches the root zone, against 50–70% overhead. Penn State puts the typical comparison at 95% against 70%. On a metered supply, or a well with a limited recovery rate, that difference settles the question on its own.

But efficiency is not the only axis, and pretending otherwise produces bad advice.

  • Plant geometry. Drip rewards fixed spacing — rows, beds, orchard lines. Broadcast plantings and pasture have no line to run tape along.
  • Water quality. Emitters clog. If the source is a pond, or a well carrying iron or fine sand, filtration stops being a $40 accessory and becomes the design constraint.
  • Cultivation. A hoe goes through drip tape without noticing. Where you cultivate mechanically, overhead survives and tape does not.
  • Frost protection. Sprinklers can carry a crop through a frost event. Drip cannot do this at all.

For most small holdings the answer is not one or the other but both, on different blocks: drip in the beds, a sprinkler or two for irregular ground and for germination, when the job is wetting a whole surface rather than a line of root zones. The full comparison works through the cases where each one wins.

What breaks first

Three failures account for most first-season disappointment, and all three are decided at install rather than in use.

No filter, or a filter in the wrong place. Emitter openings are measured in thousandths of an inch, and anything the water carries ends up there. The filter goes first in the line, ahead of the regulator, because grit chews regulator seats too.

No pressure regulator. Tape is designed for around 8–12 psi. Household and pump pressure is several times that. Without a regulator the tape balloons and splits along the seam, usually at the far end of the run — where you notice last.

Runs that are too long. Pressure falls along the line, so the last emitters deliver less than the first. The fix is not more pressure, which splits the near end, but shorter zones run in sequence on a timer.

If you would rather not assemble it yourself

Everything above is parts. The first drip system most people build fails on a mismatch — a regulator that does not suit the tape, or no filter at all — and a kit is the cheapest insurance against that. You give up flexibility and pay a little more per part; you get a set where the pressure ratings already agree.

The distinction worth knowing before you buy: a kit sized for raised beds and one sized for row crop are not interchangeable. Bed spacing and row spacing want different emitter intervals, and using the wrong one wastes water between plants for the whole season.

What it costs

Hardware for a small drip block is not expensive in absolute terms: a garden starter kit runs in the low hundreds and tape costs cents per foot. What catches people out is the split between fixtures and consumables.

Filters, regulators, mainline and fittings are bought once and last years. Tape is a consumable with a service life of one to five seasons depending on wall thickness. Budget for replacing it on a cycle rather than treating the first purchase as the cost of the system. Per-acre numbers and the three costs people forget are in what irrigation actually costs.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

Is drip always better than sprinkler?

On water use, yes — drip puts over 90% of applied water into the root zone against 50–70% for overhead sprinklers. On everything else it depends. Drip needs clean water and a filter, it suits row and tree crops with fixed spacing, and it is easy to damage with a hoe. Sprinklers tolerate dirty water, cover irregular plantings and survive cultivation, but lose water to evaporation and drift on a hot windy day.

How long does drip tape last?

One to five seasons, decided mostly by wall thickness and how the tape is handled at the end of the year. Thin-wall tape is a single-season consumable by design. Thicker tape survives being rolled up and put out again, provided the roll does not sit in sunlight over winter. Rodents are a bigger threat than UV in most places.

What pressure does a drip system need?

Most tape runs at 8–12 psi, and mains pressure is far higher than that — which is why a pressure regulator is not optional. The order is filter, then regulator, then tape. Reverse the first two and grit reaches the regulator seat; skip the regulator and the tape splits at the seam.

Can I run drip off a garden hose?

Yes, for a garden-sized block. A hose bib gives enough flow for roughly 100–200 feet of tape at a time, depending on emitter spacing and supply pressure. Beyond that the far end starves and the block should be split into zones on a timer.

How often should I water?

That is a soil question before it is a crop question. Sand holds about a third of the plant-available water that loam does, so the same crop on sand may need water daily and on loam every three or four days.
