Types of irrigation, and what each one costs you in water

Updated 17 Aug 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Irrigation methods split into three families — surface (flood and furrow, where water runs across the ground), sprinkler (hand lines, wheel lines, center pivots, spray guns) and micro (drip and micro-spray). They differ mostly in how much water reaches the root zone — surface systems run 40–90% depending on how they are managed, sprinklers 60–97% depending on the package, drip 90% and up.

Every irrigation system ever sold is a variation on one of three ideas: let water run across the ground, throw it through the air, or hand it to the plant. Everything else — the brand, the controller, the nozzle catalogue — is detail inside that choice.

The reason the choice matters is that each family loses water in a different place, and those losses are not small. Between the worst and best method in common use, more than half the water in the pipe is at stake.

Surface irrigation

Water is released at the top of the field and gravity does the distribution. No pump is strictly required, no pressure, and on ground that is already graded the capital cost is close to nothing. It is still the most used method in the world for exactly that reason.

The cost is precision. Water soaks in as it travels, so the top of the field gets a longer soak than the bottom by definition. Getting an even result means managing the stream size and the run length until the water reaches the far end before the near end has taken far more than it needs.

Extension figures put the whole family between 40 and 90 percent efficient. That range is not hedging — it is the honest difference between a graded, measured, well-timed surface system and the same field watered by opening the gate and going for lunch.

Furrow irrigation

Water runs down small parallel channels between the crop rows. The crop sits up on the ridge and drinks sideways from the furrow, which keeps the stem dry — a real agronomic advantage for anything prone to rot at the crown.

Furrow suits row crops on a consistent gentle slope. Too flat and the water never reaches the end of the run; too steep and the stream cuts the furrow apart and carries the topsoil into the tail ditch. What leaves the bottom of the field is called tailwater, and on a furrow system it is normal, not a fault — the question is whether it is captured and recirculated or lost.

Flood irrigation

The whole basin or border strip is covered. No furrows, no set-out — water is turned in at the head and spreads. On level basins with a reliable canal delivery it is efficient enough and almost free to run. On uneven ground it is the definition of watering the low spots to reach the high ones.

Flood irrigation persists because in much of the western United States the water right, the canal schedule and the field grading all assume it. Changing method means changing all three, which is a different project from buying a machine.

Sprinkler irrigation

Water goes up a pipe under pressure and comes out through nozzles above the ground. This family buys uniformity — a sprinkler does not care much about field slope — and pays for it in pumping energy and in everything the wind takes.

Hand lines and wheel lines

Aluminium pipe with impact sprinklers, moved across the field by hand or on wheels. Cheap to buy, expensive in labor, and the least efficient of the powered options at 60 to 80 percent. Impact sprinklers throwing above the crop lose a quarter to a third of the water to drift and evaporation before it lands.

Center pivot irrigation

A lateral several hundred feet long walks in a circle around a fixed point. The standard field is a quarter section — half a mile by half a mile, 160 acres — with the circle inscribed inside the square. Corners stay dry unless a corner arm or an end gun handles them, and an end gun on a 160-acre field reaches an extra 8 to 10 acres, throwing up to 120 feet, at the worst uniformity of anything on the machine.

The pivot itself is not what decides efficiency. The nozzle package is:

  • MESA, mid-elevation spray on drops, is the common default and lands about 78 percent of the water where it is wanted;
  • LESA, the same drops taken lower, about 88 percent;
  • LEPA, heads down at crop level applying in bubbles rather than spray, about 95;
  • MDI, drip tubing dragged along the ground by the machine, about 97.

That is a 19-point spread on the same steel, in the same field, in the same season. It is the cheapest efficiency available to anyone who already owns a pivot.

Spray irrigation: guns and travelers

A single large nozzle, often on a cart pulled across the field by its own hose. Simple, mobile, and by a distance the leakiest thing in the catalogue: 35 to 40 percent of the water is lost to drift and evaporation before it reaches the soil. Their case is coverage of an odd-shaped block for a short season, not efficiency.

Micro irrigation

Water is delivered at low pressure directly at the plant, through tape, line or micro-sprays. Nothing is thrown through the air, so nothing is lost to wind, and the wetted area is only the root zone rather than the whole surface. Extension figures start at 90 percent and go up.

The conditions on that number matter more than in any other family. Drip depends on filtration, on pressure regulation, and on someone noticing when emitters block. A drip system nobody maintains is not a 90 percent system — it is a full-price system watering half the field.

What an irrigation system costs

Method decides the shape of the bill, not just its size. A surface system on ground that is already graded is mostly labor and gated pipe. A sprinkler system is pump, mainline and heads, and the pump is sized by the whole block running at once. Drip is the cheapest per foot and the most expensive in parts you did not think about — filter, regulator, header, fittings — which is why our irrigation cost page prices the hardware line by line rather than quoting a figure per acre.

For a DIY irrigation system on a small holding the order of spending is the same every time: filtration first, pressure regulation second, distribution third, automation last. A timer on an unfiltered drip line automates the clogging.

Choosing between them

For a small holding the honest ranking is not by efficiency but by what the failure looks like. Surface irrigation fails slowly and visibly — you can see the water. Sprinklers fail loudly, with a broken head spraying at the sky. Drip fails silently, and the first sign is a row that stopped growing two weeks ago.

Match the method to how often you will actually walk the system, then read the efficiency table as a ceiling rather than a promise. The gap between a well-run surface field and a neglected drip system is wider than the gap between the methods on paper.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

What are the main types of irrigation?

Three families. Surface irrigation moves water across the field by gravity — flood and furrow are the common forms. Sprinkler irrigation throws it through the air — hand lines, wheel lines, center pivots, linear moves and traveling guns. Micro irrigation delivers it at the plant — drip tape, drip line and micro-sprays. Everything sold under a brand name is a variation inside one of those three.

What is furrow irrigation?

A form of surface irrigation where water runs down small parallel channels cut between crop rows and soaks in sideways and down as it advances. The crop sits on the ridge, the water runs in the furrow. It needs a field with a consistent gentle slope, and its efficiency depends almost entirely on getting the stream size and the run length right.

What is flood irrigation?

Surface irrigation where a whole basin or border strip is covered with water rather than being fed down individual furrows. It is the oldest method still in commercial use, the cheapest to install where water is delivered by canal, and the least precise — parts of the field get more than they need so that the far end gets enough.

What is center pivot irrigation?

A sprinkler lateral, several hundred feet long, that rotates around a fixed point in the middle of the field. Standard geometry is a quarter section — half a mile square, 160 acres — with the pivot sweeping a circle inside it. The corners stay dry unless a corner arm or an end gun covers them.

Which type of irrigation is most efficient?

Drip, on paper and usually in practice — extension figures put it at 90% and above, against 80–97% for a well-specified center pivot and 40–90% for surface systems. But the spread inside each method is wider than the gap between methods — a badly managed drip system loses to a well-managed pivot.

Is spray irrigation the same as sprinkler irrigation?

In everyday use, yes — both mean throwing water through the air. Inside the industry "spray" usually points at the nozzle package rather than the machine — mid-elevation spray application on a pivot, as opposed to impact sprinklers above the crop or LEPA heads dropped down near the ground.

What is an irrigation system?

The set of parts that takes water from a source and puts a measured amount of it in the root zone — a pump or a gravity head, a filter, a pressure regulator where the emitters need one, a mainline, and whatever does the applying at the end. The applicator is what people name the system after, but the parts ahead of it decide whether it works.

Does the method decide how much water I use?

Less than the scheduling does. Method sets the ceiling on efficiency; when and how long you run it decides how close you get to that ceiling. The largest single saving on most holdings is not a new system but watering to a measured soil moisture deficit.
