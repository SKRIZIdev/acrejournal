Irrigation hardware is cheap in absolute terms and structured in a way that catches people out. The mistake is not overspending on the first purchase — it is treating the first purchase as the cost of the system.

Fixtures and consumables

Split every price list into two columns before you compare anything.

Fixtures, bought once and lasting years: filters, pressure regulators, mainline, fittings, valves, timers, sprinkler stands and heads. This is where the quality decision belongs, because replacing them is disruptive.

Consumables, on a replacement cycle: drip tape, above all. Depending on wall thickness tape lasts one season to five. Thin-wall tape is designed as an annual item; heavier tape survives being rolled and redeployed if it is not left in sunlight over winter.

A budget that lists only fixtures looks cheap and is wrong by whatever the tape costs every few years.

Why per-acre pricing misleads

Ask what a drip system costs per acre and every answer is wrong, because area is not what drives the cost. Row spacing is.

An acre of orchard rows twelve feet apart needs roughly 3,600 feet of line. An acre of close-planted beds at three feet needs four times that. Same acre, four times the tape, four times the emitters, four times the replacement cycle.

Price a system in feet of line and number of zones. Anyone quoting you a dollars-per-acre figure without asking about spacing is guessing.

The three costs people leave out

Filtration sized to your water. The filter that ships with a kit assumes clean supply. Pond water, surface water and iron-bearing wells need more, and finding that out after the emitters clog costs you a season of growth, not just the filter.

Fittings. Every system needs more couplings, tees, end caps and goof plugs than the plan says. Buy a surplus with the first order; a $2 fitting that stops the job on a Saturday costs a day.

The replacement cycle. Divide the tape cost by its service life in seasons and put that number in the annual budget. On thin-wall tape it is the largest recurring line in the whole system.

Where the money is actually saved

Not in cheaper hardware. In three decisions:

  1. Zone properly. Long runs starve at the far end and tempt you to raise pressure, which splits tape at the near end. Shorter zones on a timer cost one timer and save the tape.
  2. Filter before the regulator. Grit chews regulator seats. Getting the order right costs nothing and saves a component.
  3. Buy tape by wall thickness, not by price per foot. The cheapest tape is the most expensive over five seasons unless you genuinely want an annual consumable.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

What does a drip system cost per acre?

There is no single figure, because the cost scales with row spacing rather than area. A block of widely spaced rows needs a fraction of the tape that close-planted beds do on the same acre. Price it by feet of tape and number of zones, not by acreage.

What is the biggest recurring cost?

Tape. Depending on wall thickness it lasts one to five seasons, and it is the only part of a well-built system that is designed to be thrown away. Filters, regulators, mainline and fittings are bought once.

Do I need a timer?

Once you have more than one zone, yes — not for convenience but because zones have to run in sequence to hold pressure. Without one you are the timer, every day, for the whole season.

What do people forget to budget for?

Three things. Filtration sized to the actual water source rather than to the catalogue default. Enough fittings, because you always need more than you counted. And the replacement cycle for tape, which turns a one-off purchase into an annual line item.

Is a kit cheaper than buying parts separately?

Not usually on price. The reason to buy a kit is that the pressure ratings of the parts agree, which is exactly the thing a first-time buyer gets wrong when assembling a system from a parts list.
