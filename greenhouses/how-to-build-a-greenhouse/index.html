How to build a greenhouse

Updated 16 Aug 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Start with your county's ground snow load, not with a design. Snow and span are what collapse tunnels, and every other decision follows from them. A frame kit runs from about $1,589 for a 16 ft span to $2,630 for 24 ft; covering, end walls and ground anchoring are extra and are where most first-time budgets fall short.

Most greenhouse guides start with a shopping list. That is the wrong end. A greenhouse fails in one night, under snow, and the decisions that prevent it are made before anything is bought.

Step 1: find your snow load

Your county publishes a ground snow load figure in local building code, derived from the ASCE hazard maps. That number, not the catalogue, decides what you can build.

Commercial designs are commonly rated to around 35 lb per square foot of snow and 100 mph of wind. That is a manufacturing convention — it tells you what a typical structure is built to, not whether it suits your site.

Two things make snow worse than the headline figure suggests. Span: a wider hoop carries more load on the same frame, so a design offered in 16 ft and 24 ft is not equally strong in both. Drifting: snow slides down the arch and piles at the shoulder, and that is where tunnels actually deform — not at the ridge.

Step 2: choose the span and the height

Width is what you notice in the catalogue. Height is what you notice every day you work in it.

A 16 ft low-profile package lists at $1,588.95 and a 16 ft high tunnel at $1,920.95 — about $330 apart. That $330 buys sidewall height, and sidewall height is what makes the outer beds reachable. Those beds are roughly a quarter of your growing area, so a low shoulder means paying for space you then cannot use properly.

Going wider is the more expensive move: 20 ft high tunnel at $2,390.95, 24 ft low-profile at $2,629.95. Twenty feet is the common working size for a market garden — a center path with beds either side. Twenty-four is where machinery fits, and where you must check the snow rating rather than assume it.

Step 3: pick the covering

The frame outlives the covering several times over, so this is a recurring cost rather than a one-off.

Covering Service life Where it makes sense
Co-poly film About 1 season Overwintering, temporary structures
Greenhouse-grade poly 4 years warranted The default for working tunnels
Twin-wall polycarbonate 10–15 years Cold climates, structures you keep
Corrugated polycarbonate Up to 20 years Permanent builds

These service lives come from university extension material — the UMass Amherst film fact sheet and Oklahoma State's guide. They are the extension services' figures, not our measurements, and in a high-UV state expect the real number to fall short of the warranty.

Run the maths over fifteen years rather than over one purchase: four rounds of greenhouse-grade poly against one polycarbonate install is closer than the shelf prices suggest, and re-covering costs labor as well as material.

Step 4: what the kit does not include

This is where first budgets break. A kit is a frame, sometimes with covering. Plan separately for:

  • Ground anchoring — posts driven to depth, or a base. This is what holds the structure down in wind.
  • End walls and a door wide enough for a wheelbarrow or a cart. Two end walls are a surprising share of the total build.
  • Ventilation — roll-up sides or fans. A closed tunnel in summer cooks the crop.
  • Covering, if the kit is frame-only, plus batten or wiggle wire to hold it.
  • Repair tape, about $21, which postpones a full recover when a tear appears.

How to build a greenhouse cheaply

Three approaches genuinely reduce cost without reducing the structure to scrap:

Buy a DIY kit and source materials locally. These supply the fittings and connectors — the parts that are hard to improvise — and let you buy pipe and timber near you rather than shipping them.

Build a hoop house rather than a greenhouse. Cattle panel or PVC hoops over a timber base make a legitimate season-extension structure for a fraction of the price. Accept what it is: PVC bends under snow and degrades in UV, so it is a two-to-four year structure and should be uncovered before a heavy winter.

Buy height instead of width. As above — $330 for a taller sidewall does more for usable space than the same money spent on span.

What does not work is under-specifying the frame for your snow load, or spacing the hoops wider than the design says to save on materials. Both save money once and cost the whole structure later.

Step 5: check it before you build

Our greenhouse calculator takes your span, hoop spacing and local snow load and tells you when the combination does not hold — including when the covering you picked cannot carry the snow you specified. That combination is the common failure, and no catalogue flags it for you.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

How much does it cost to build a greenhouse?

A frame kit is $1,589 for a 16 ft low-profile span and $2,630 for 24 ft at current seller prices, checked 16 August 2026. On top of that: covering from about $51 for 4-year 6 mil film, end walls, a door, ground posts or a base, and ventilation. A realistic small working tunnel lands well above the kit price, and the covering comes back as a cost every few years.

How to build a greenhouse cheap?

Three routes actually work. Buy a DIY kit that supplies the fittings and source the timber and pipe locally. Build a hoop house over cattle panel or PVC for a season-extension structure rather than a permanent one. Or buy a smaller span with a taller sidewall instead of a wider low one — height costs less than width and makes the edge beds usable. What does not work is under-specifying the frame for your snow load.

How to make a greenhouse without buying a kit?

Same three steps in the same order — snow load, span, covering — but you source the frame yourself. Cattle panel bent over a timber base, or PVC hoops in a mild-winter county, will make a greenhouse for a fraction of a kit price. The fittings are the part worth buying rather than improvising.

How to build a small greenhouse?

Smaller is easier structurally and harder ergonomically. A short span carries snow better, but a low shoulder wastes the outer beds — so on a small greenhouse the sidewall height matters even more than on a large one. Under about 12 ft of span, build tall or accept that only the center is usable.

How much is a greenhouse?

A frame kit runs $1,589 to $2,630 depending on span at current seller prices. What a greenhouse costs in total is that plus covering, end walls, a door, ground anchoring and ventilation — realistically well above the kit figure, and with the covering returning as a cost every few years.

Can I build a greenhouse out of PVC?

For a light season-extension structure in a mild-winter county, yes, and it is the cheapest frame there is. PVC bends under snow and degrades in UV, so it is not a structure you leave covered through a heavy winter. Treat it as a two-to-four year structure, not a decade one.

Does a greenhouse kit include the foundation?

Almost never. Kits are frame, and sometimes covering. Ground posts, base boards, end walls, doors, concrete and ventilation are usually extra or your own work. Read the parts list before comparing two kit prices — the cheaper one often stops earlier.

What size greenhouse should I build?

Wider than you think for growing, and taller than you think for working. The step from a 16 ft low-profile to a 16 ft high tunnel is about $330 and it is the upgrade people are gladdest they made: sidewall height is what makes the edge beds usable, and edge beds are a quarter of the growing area.

Which way should a greenhouse face?

In most of the US, orient the ridge north to south for even light through the day on a season-long crop. An east-west ridge favours low winter sun, which suits a structure used mainly for overwintering. Site it out of the prevailing wind and away from anything that drops snow onto it.
