Size my greenhouse

A greenhouse is the one structure on a small holding that fails all at once. Irrigation degrades, fences sag, but a tunnel stands through four winters and then goes down in a single night with a crop inside it.

Which is why this section starts with the load and works outwards. Everything else — glazing, ventilation, benching — is a choice. The snow is not.

Start with the load, not the catalogue

Snow load is set by your county, published in local building code and derived from the ASCE ground snow load maps. Commercial greenhouse designs are commonly rated to around 35 lb per square foot of snow and 100 mph of wind — a figure quoted by Oklahoma State Extension — but that is a manufacturing convention, not a guarantee that it suits your site.

Two things make the number worse than it looks. Span: a wider hoop carries more load on the same frame, so the same design in 16 ft and 24 ft widths is not equally strong. Drifting: snow slides down the arch and piles at the shoulder, which is where tunnels actually deform, not at the ridge.

Our greenhouse calculator works from your span, spacing and local load, and it will tell you when the covering you picked cannot carry the snow you specified — because that combination is the common one, and no catalogue flags it.

Which covering

The choice looks like a price comparison and is really a time horizon.

Covering Service life Where it makes sense
Co-poly film About 1 season Overwintering structures, temporary high tunnels
Greenhouse-grade poly 4 years warranted The default for working tunnels; costs about double co-poly
Twin-wall polycarbonate 10–15 years Cold climates, heated houses, structures you keep
Corrugated polycarbonate Up to 20 years Permanent structures where the glazing outlives the plan

Those service lives come from university extension material — the UMass Amherst greenhouse film fact sheet and Oklahoma State's guide to structures and coverings. These are the extension services' figures, not our measurements — we cite them rather than test them. Expect the real number to fall short of a warranty in a high-UV state.

Run that over fifteen years rather than over one purchase. Four rounds of greenhouse-grade poly against one polycarbonate install is a much closer contest than the shelf price suggests, and the labor of re-covering is not free either.

Two practical notes that do not appear on spec sheets. UV-stabilised film yellows and cracks more slowly, and it is worth the premium in high-sun states. And most premature film failures start where the sheet rubs on the frame — tape the contact points before you tension, not after you find the hole.

If a packaged kit is more than you need

Those prices assume you want a finished structure delivered. Plenty of people covering one or two beds do not, and the range below starts an order of magnitude lower — a low tunnel is $36, a 16 ft hoop house you assemble yourself is $718 against $1,589 for the packaged version of the same span. What you pay for in the packaged kit is the ground posts, the hardware and not having to work out the build order yourself.

The caterpillar tunnel at the top of the range is worth understanding even if you buy neither: no end walls, cover held by rope over the hoops, moved by hand between seasons. It is the cheapest way to put real growing area under cover, and it is why market gardens use them instead of buying a second greenhouse.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

Polycarbonate or film?

Film is the cheaper entry and the more frequent job. Greenhouse-grade polyethylene is warranted around four years; single-season co-poly is cheaper still and lasts one. Twin-wall polycarbonate runs ten to fifteen years, and corrugated polycarbonate can reach twenty. Over a fifteen-year horizon the totals are closer than the shelf prices suggest, which is why the real question is how long you intend to keep the structure.

Does a kit include the foundation?

Almost never. Kits are frame and covering; ground posts, base boards, doors, end walls and any concrete are usually extra or your own work. Read the parts list before comparing two kit prices, because the cheaper one often stops earlier.

How much snow does a tunnel need to carry?

That is a local code question, not a catalogue question. Commercial designs are available rated to about 35 lb per square foot of snow and 100 mph wind, but your county's ground snow load is the number that matters. Span makes it worse — a wider hoop carries more load on the same frame.

Can I leave the covering on over winter?

If the structure is rated for your snow load and the covering is tensioned properly, yes. If it is not, the usual failure sequence is snow sliding to the shoulder, the hoop deforming, and the film tearing along the frame line. Growers in heavy-snow counties often strip the film in autumn on unheated tunnels rather than gamble.

What actually kills greenhouse film?

Ultraviolet light and abrasion, in that order. UV-stabilised products yellow and crack more slowly, and most premature failures happen where the film rubs on the frame. Repair tape on a small tear is worth doing the same week — a tear left alone grows along the tension line until the sheet is scrap.
