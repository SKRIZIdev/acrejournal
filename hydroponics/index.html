LIGHT IS THE BUDGET WATTS ARE THE BILL

Work out my lighting

This is the smallest section on the site, and that is a decision rather than an oversight. Indoor growing carries a fraction of the search demand of tractors or greenhouses, and the part of it we can write about honestly is smaller still.

What we can do here is fix the one mistake that costs beginners the most money: buying light by wattage.

Light is a quantity, not a power rating

Plants do not respond to watts. They respond to how many photons land on a square meter over a day — the daily light integral, measured in moles per square meter per day.

That distinction has a practical consequence. A fixture's wattage tells you what the meter will register; DLI tells you whether the crop will actually produce. Leafy greens are content at roughly 12–17 mol/m²/day, and fruiting crops want more than double. A modest fixture running eighteen hours can deliver more usable light than a powerful one running eight, at lower cost.

Our grow light calculator works from the target DLI for your crop and photoperiod, converts it to the light you need over your footprint, and warns you when the fixture you are considering substantially overshoots. Overshoot is not harmless — it is money spent on electricity and heat you then have to remove.

Complete system or piece by piece

Buying separately is cheaper on paper and works if you already know what you are doing. The failure it invites is mismatch: a fan that cannot move the air the light heats, a tent that does not take the fixture's mounting, a controller that speaks to neither.

A matched system removes that class of problem, and for a first grow that is worth the premium. The step up in size costs less than people expect — a 2x4 at $679 against a 2x2 at $499 — and the extra footprint is usually the thing you wish you had bought.

Lights on their own

If you already have a tent and are replacing the fixture, size by DLI first and then check the physical fit and hanging height. Bar-style fixtures spread light more evenly than a single panel, and that becomes visible once the canopy is wider than about three feet.

What it costs to run

The purchase price is the small number. Running cost is fixture wattage times hours, divided by 1,000, times your electricity rate — and then add roughly a third again for fans and dehumidification.

A 200 W light on an eighteen-hour photoperiod is about 108 kWh a month before the supporting gear. At typical US residential rates that is a real monthly line item, and it is why sizing by DLI rather than by "more is better" pays back every month rather than once.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

How much light do leafy greens need?

Lettuce and most greens do well at a daily light integral of roughly 12–17 mol per square meter per day; fruiting crops such as tomatoes want more than double that. DLI combines intensity and hours, which is why a modest fixture running eighteen hours can beat a powerful one running eight.

Why not just shop by watts?

Because watts measure what you pay the utility, not what the plant receives. Two 400 W fixtures can differ substantially in usable light depending on diodes and driver efficiency. Size by DLI for the crop and the photoperiod, then check that the wattage fits your circuit.

What does a starter setup cost?

A complete 2x2 system with integrated controls starts around $499, and a 4x4 around $999 at current AC Infinity list prices. Buying separately can come in lower, but the saving disappears the first time a fan is mismatched to the tent and you buy twice.

Do I need a controller?

For one tent, not strictly — a timer and a thermometer will do. Controllers earn their place when fans, lights and humidity have to respond to each other, which is where most grows go wrong in week three rather than week one.

How much electricity will it use?

Multiply the fixture wattage by hours per day and divide by 1,000 to get kilowatt hours, then apply your rate. A 200 W light on eighteen hours is 3.6 kWh a day — roughly 108 kWh a month before fans and dehumidification, which typically add a third again.
