THE SECOND OWNER PAYS FOR THE FIRST YEAR

Biggest by demand

Buying a used compact tractor — what hours actually tell you, what depreciation does in the first year, and the checks that decide whether a machine is worth the asking price.

Class, age, hours and condition — get the price range a used machine is actually worth before you negotiate.

Value this machine

Used is where most compact tractors actually change hands, and it is the part of the market with the least honest information in it. New machines have a manufacturer's specification page. Used machines have an asking price, a photograph and a number on an hour meter.

This section is about closing that gap: what the hour meter really tells you, how much of the new price has already evaporated, and what twenty minutes of inspection can rule out.

What hours actually mean

The instinct is to read the hour meter like a car's odometer, and it does not work that way. Two figures matter more than the total.

Hours per year. A compact tractor doing under about 100 hours a year is a homeowner machine — mowing, a bit of loader work, snow. That is most of this market. The same total compressed into two or three years means the machine worked for a living, and a hard-working compact wears the hydraulics and the loader mounts, not the engine.

What the hours were. Loader cycles are hard on a machine in a way that hours in a field are not: constant direction changes, shock loads through the front axle, and weight carried ahead of the wheels. A tractor that spent its life on a mower deck at 1,400 hours can be in better shape than one that spent 600 hours moving gravel.

Ask what the machine did. If the seller cannot say, that is itself an answer.

What it is worth

Depreciation on equipment follows the same shape as on vehicles: steep at first, then flattening. The first-year drop is the largest single number in the whole transaction, and it is the reason a one-to-three-year-old machine is usually the best value in the market — the first owner has absorbed the delivery, the prep and the retail margin on the loader package, and the machine still has a documented service history.

Our value calculator runs age and hours against the new price to produce a range. Two warnings about it. The depreciation curve is modelled rather than surveyed, so it is a negotiating anchor, not an appraisal. And it cannot see condition — a well-kept machine at the top of the range beats a neglected one at the bottom every time.

One generation note that is currently costing buyers money: the Kubota L2501 has been replaced by the L2502, and listings mix the two freely. They are close in specification but not identical, and parts are not interchangeable across the board. Check which one you are actually looking at before comparing prices.

The twenty-minute inspection

In order, because each check makes the next one meaningful.

  1. Cold start. Insist on it. A machine warmed up before you arrive is hiding hard starting, smoke on start-up, or both.
  2. Hydraulics under load. Raise the loader to full height with something in the bucket and hold it. Drift means worn seals or a tired pump — the most expensive common repair on a compact.
  3. Three-point hitch, full travel. Cycle it up and down. Uneven lift arms, a hitch that will not hold position, or a leaking cylinder all show here.
  4. Loader mounts and frame. Look for weld repairs, elongated pin holes and cracks around the subframe. This is where abuse shows first.
  5. Fluids. Milky oil means water. Metal glitter in hydraulic fluid means something is grinding itself apart.
  6. Tyres and rims. Fluid-filled rear tyres are normal ballast; rust streaks down the rim mean a slow leak of that fluid.
  7. Service records. Not decisive on their own, but their complete absence on a dealer-sold machine is a question worth asking out loud.

Financing works here too

Most people assume equipment finance is a new-machine product. It is not, and on a used purchase the arithmetic is often better: a shorter term on a smaller sum, with the steep first-year depreciation already paid by someone else.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

How many hours is too many on a compact tractor?

The number alone tells you very little. A machine with 1,200 hours over twelve years has been used gently and maintained on a schedule; the same 1,200 hours over two years means commercial work. On compacts, the more useful signal is hours per year — under about 100 is light homeowner use, and that is the majority of this market.

Is a used tractor with a loader worth the premium?

Usually, because retrofitting one costs more than the price difference and the factory-matched loader fits properly. What to check is whether the loader was matched to that model or adapted, and whether the frame shows repair around the mounts.

Why is the first year of depreciation so steep?

For the same reason it is on a car — the second owner is not paying for delivery, dealer prep or the loader package's retail margin. That is also the argument for buying at one to three years old, where most of the drop has happened and the machine still has a warranty history.

Should I buy from a dealer or a private seller?

A dealer machine costs more and comes with recourse, service records and often a limited warranty. A private machine is cheaper and everything is on you. For a first tractor, where you cannot yet tell a worn hydraulic pump by sound, the dealer premium is cheap insurance.

What about auctions?

Auction prices look attractive until you count what you cannot do — start it under load, drive it, or return it. They suit buyers who can assess a machine cold and absorb a bad one. If that is not you, the saving is not real.
