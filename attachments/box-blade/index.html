What is a box blade, and what size do you need?

Updated 17 Aug 2026 · 6 min read
Short answer

A box blade is a three-sided steel box on the three-point hitch with scarifier teeth in front of a cutting edge. It levels, spreads and grades — pulling material along rather than pushing it aside. Size it by weight against your hitch capacity first, and by width second.

A box blade is the least glamorous implement on the lot and the one most owners of a compact tractor end up using most. It is a three-sided steel box that hangs off the three-point hitch, with a row of scarifier teeth ahead of a cutting edge.

It does something no other rear implement does: it cuts material loose, carries it, and lets it out where the ground is low.

What it actually does

Three functions in one pass.

The scarifiers rip the surface. On a gravel drive that has been packed by two winters of traffic, nothing will enter the box until the top inch is broken, and that is what the teeth are for. On soft ground you raise them out of the way.

The front cutting edge fills the box as you pull forward. Material collects, and the box carries it until the ground drops away.

The rear edge levels. What spills out behind is dragged flat by the trailing edge, which is why a box blade leaves a surface rather than a pile. A rear blade cannot do this — it angles material off to one side and keeps none of it.

Sizing: weight first, width second

Everyone sizes a box blade by width and gets it wrong. Width decides whether you cover the tyre tracks — if the blade is narrower than the rear track you drive over your own work and undo it. That sets the minimum, usually 48 to 60 inches on a 25 hp machine.

Weight decides whether you can lift it at all. A 60 inch box blade with scarifiers runs roughly 400 to 500 lb empty, and the hitch rating you need to look at is the figure measured 24 inches behind the hitch, not at the lift arm ends. On a Kubota L2502 at 1,389 lb there is plenty of room. On a John Deere 1025R at 681 lb there is not — that machine wants a light 48 inch blade or none at all.

The implement matcher checks both limits in the right order.

The ballast argument

On a light tractor, the box blade earns its place before you use it.

A loader lifts against the front axle. Every pound in the bucket tries to rotate the machine forward, and the counterweight is whatever sits behind the rear axle. A sub-compact with a loaded bucket and nothing on the hitch will get light at the back, and on a slope that is how people get hurt.

A box blade on the hitch is 400 lb of ballast that you can also grade with. Fill it with gravel and it is more. This is why the standard advice in owner forums — buy the box blade with the loader, not later — keeps repeating.

What it will not do

It will not dig a hole; the box has to move forward to fill. It will not maintain a crowned road as well as a land plane, because a land plane floats and a box blade cuts. And it will not fix a drive that has no material left in it: a box blade redistributes what is there, it does not add gravel.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

What does a box blade do?

It cuts, carries and drops material in one pass. The scarifiers break the surface, the front edge fills the box, and the trailing edge levels what falls out behind. That is the difference from a rear blade, which pushes material to the side and cannot hold any. Gravel drives, rough ground after a build, spreading a load of topsoil — box blade work.

What size box blade for a compact tractor?

Match the width to the tractor's rear track so the blade covers the tyre tracks, then check the weight against your hitch. On a 25 hp class machine that is normally 48 to 60 inches. A 60 inch box blade weighs roughly 400 to 500 lb, which is inside a 1,389 lb hitch and outside a 681 lb one.

Do I need scarifiers?

On anything that has been driven on, yes. Compacted gravel and dry clay will not enter the box without being broken first — without teeth the blade skids across the surface and polishes it. Adjustable or removable teeth are worth having, because on soft material they dig in too much.

Can a box blade be used as ballast?

Yes, and on a sub-compact that is half the reason to own one. A loaded loader lifts the rear of a light tractor; weight behind the axle holds it down. A box blade parked on the hitch is ballast you can also work with, unlike a ballast box full of concrete.

Box blade or land plane?

A land plane rides on skids and floats material into low spots, which makes it better at maintaining an already-good driveway. A box blade cuts, which makes it better at fixing a bad one. If you have potholes and a crown to rebuild, box blade; if you have washboard on a decent surface, land plane.
